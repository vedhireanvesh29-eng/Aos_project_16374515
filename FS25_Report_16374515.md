## Analysis
FCFS records 68.13 units of waiting and 79.67 units of turnaround while keeping the CPU busy 100.00% of the 173-unit horizon. Arrival-then-PID tie-breakers maintain order, yet the convoy effect remains: once a long burst enters the 0.09 jobs-per-unit pipeline, every follower idles behind it.

SJF trims the queue to 45.47 waiting and 57.00 turnaround units by always dispatching the smallest available burst. Earlier arrivals and smaller numeric PIDs still win ties, so short tasks finish sooner without starving simultaneous arrivals, though long jobs must still exercise patience.

SRTF pushes responsiveness further by preempting whenever a shorter remaining burst appears. Waiting drops to 44.73 units and response time to 42.53 units while throughput stays at 0.09. The policy incurs 16 context switches, yet the additional dispatcher work keeps the CPU serving whoever currently benefits most.

Priority scheduling mirrors FCFS structure but biases urgency: average waiting sits at 70.07 units with only 14 switches. High-priority tasks leap to the front once the CPU is idle, whereas low-priority tasks can still languish when urgent arrivals arrive back-to-back, highlighting starvation risk in a non-preemptive design.

Round Robin enforces fairness. With a four-unit quantum, response time settles at 25.20 units and waiting at 90.00 units, accompanied by 47 context switches. No job monopolises the CPU, and adjusting the quantum would tilt the balance: a smaller slice improves responsiveness at the cost of more switching, while a larger slice drifts toward FCFS behaviour.

Viewed together, the baseline metrics confirm that scheduling strategy shapes latency rather than makespan. FCFS and priority minimise dispatcher effort, SJF and SRTF minimise queueing, and Round Robin trades a moderate number of switches for visible fairness across users.

## Transient Impact

When PX arrives at t=10, FCFS experiences an increase of 1.49 waiting time units and an increase of 1.00 context switches. Without preemption the emergency job must watch the current burst finish, so the convoy effect intensifies exactly when fast accommodation is required.

SRTF and Round Robin adapt quickly. SRTF reports an increase of 2.84 response time units alongside a decrease of 1.00 context switches, proving the value of remaining-time comparisons. Round Robin shows an increase of 0.93 response time units with an increase of 2.00 context switches, while the priority scheduler still suffers an increase of 1.00 waiting time units because an executing medium-priority task is never interrupted.

## Conclusion
SRTF is the best overall policy for this workload: it delivers the lowest response times, preserves throughput, and absorbs the transient job with minimal disruption. Round Robin is the most balanced alternative when fairness matters more than raw latency. FCFS and priority remain suitable only when implementation simplicity or strict priority ordering outweigh the need for reaction speed.
